== Through-line

The clinical AI that actually delivers value is small, predictive and local. Hospitals can neither
_run_ it -- they were never built to compute -- nor _approve_ it, because governance was written
for a different machine: the LLM. The fix is infrastructure, and governance that can tell the
difference.

Candidate quotable lines:

* "Forget AI -- most hospitals can't do any advanced computing."
* "A 100 MB model that reads images and writes images gets asked about hallucination and leaking
  PHI to big model companies. There is no text. There is no leak."
* "Do not move the data. Move the computation."

== The argument

=== 1. Current state of "AI" in hospitals

*Rudolph's point.* There isn't really any -- not in the Northeast, and certainly not at places like
Boston Children's. Rudolph deployed the only semi-production AI workflows in clinical radiology at
BCH, and they are predictive. BCH uses generative AI, but not on clinically load-bearing work. Most
generative effort goes where LLMs plausibly fit: interpreting free-text clinical notes.

*Evidence -- 2026 (on the slides).*

* *Adoption*: 75% of US health systems use at least one AI application, up from 59% in 2025;
  clinical note-taking is the top use at 68% cite:[eliciting2026] (secondary source). 81% of
  physicians use AI professionally, up from 38% in 2023 cite:[ama2026].
* *Cleared AI is still imaging, and none of it is tagged as LLM-based*: the FDA list holds 1,614
  devices (latest decision 29 Jun 2026), 1,230 (76%) radiology; no entry is tagged as
  foundation-model or LLM-based, and the FDA says it will "explore methods to identify and tag" them
  cite:[fda_ai_devices_2026] (counted from the list 2026-10-16).
* *Capability vs deployment*: an autonomous agent beat specialists on simulated emergency cases
  (87.8% vs 78.1% on a 311-case subset) cite:[ferber2026mira], but on 100 real-EHR tasks the best of
  13 agents finished 46% cite:[liu2026physicianbench] (preprint). LLM assistance now improves
  clinicians in some trials (57.5% vs 37.2% top-3 accuracy in nephrology, with GPT-5 alone at
  77.1%) cite:[bentegeac2026]; see also cite:[qazi2026]. Capability is arriving; load-bearing
  deployment is not.

*Evidence -- background (2023--2024 data).*

* *Predictive AI is widespread but mostly vendor-supplied and EHR-embedded*: 71% of US hospitals
  used EHR-integrated predictive AI in 2024 cite:[astp2025], typically for inpatient risk and
  scheduling. By source in 2024: 80% use models from their EHR vendor, 52% from third parties, and
  50% models they built themselves (categories overlap) cite:[astp2025]; 2023 figures were similar
  cite:[nong2025]. So predictive, yes; but mostly vendor-supplied, with self-built models a large
  minority (half of hospitals run at least one). Growth in third-party and self-built models is
  concentrated in health systems cite:[astp2025].
* *On "mostly self-built"*: Rudolph recalls a recent study finding hospital AI overwhelmingly
  predictive _and_ mostly built in-house by hospital scientists and engineers. Searched 2026-10-16
  and not found. The national data says the opposite on sourcing (above). It may hold for academic
  centres -- self-development and local evaluation skew to well-resourced, system-affiliated
  hospitals cite:[nong2025] -- but no study isolating academic centres turned up. A Lancet Digital
  Health viewpoint (2026) makes the related regulatory point: models built in-house by a health
  system can reach patients without FDA review. Usable on stage: "half of US hospitals run AI they
  built themselves" -- and those models need compute and governance.
* *Recency*: the newest national figures describe 2024 (ASTP brief Sep 2025; Everson et al.
  Dec 2025). The AHA survey lags about 18 months; no 2025-data release was found.
* *Cleared clinical AI is overwhelmingly imaging*: roughly three-quarters of FDA AI device
  authorizations are radiology cite:[fda_ai_devices] (trade-press count; verify before a slide).
* *Generative AI in hospitals is text and documentation*: 31.5% of hospitals had EHR-integrated
  generative AI in 2024 cite:[everson2025]; in a 43-system survey, ambient note-taking was the only
  use case every system was pursuing cite:[poon2025].
* *Generative AI is not doing load-bearing clinical reasoning*: on 2,400 real cases, LLMs did worse
  than physicians and ignored guidelines -- "not ready for autonomous clinical decision-making"
  cite:[hager2024]; giving physicians GPT-4 did not improve their diagnoses cite:[goh2024].

*Can LLMs analyse images? Is there a generative tech that does?* Short answer: not reliably, and
diffusion models do something else.

* *2026 (on the slides)*: RadLE 2.0 (Jul 2026, 200 cases, 16 models incl. GPT-5.6 Sol Pro, Gemini 3
  Pro, Claude Fable 5): on the confidence-weighted index human experts scored 988.7/2000, the best
  model 758; none reached the human baseline, and several models made many high-confidence errors
  cite:[radle2_2026]. Gemini 3 Pro reportedly had the highest raw accuracy, "nearly catching up" on
  hit rate -- the gap is now calibration as much as accuracy.
* _Background (2024--25):_

* Multimodal LLMs are much weaker on images than on text. GPT-4V scored 81.5% on text-only radiology
  board questions but 47.8% on image questions, with hallucinated findings cite:[hayden2024]. On 50
  expert spot-diagnosis cases, GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro scored about 0.30 against 0.83 for
  board-certified radiologists cite:[datta2025radle] (preprint).
* Diffusion models are the image-generating counterpart. In medical imaging they are used mostly
  for synthesis and data augmentation, denoising, reconstruction, super-resolution, image-to-image
  translation and some anomaly detection cite:[kazerouni2023] -- making or cleaning images, not
  reading them.
* Image _interpretation_ that works -- detection, segmentation, quantification -- is still the
  discriminative, predictive kind. That is section 2's point.

*How a "language" model sees an image (mechanism, for Q&A).* The image is cut into small patches
(on the order of 14--16 px square); a vision encoder -- a transformer, usually pretrained to match
internet images with their captions -- turns each patch into a vector; those vectors are projected
into the LLM's input space and sit in the sequence alongside text tokens. From there it is the same
generative machine: the model samples text conditioned on everything in the sequence. So yes -- the
image is tokenized and then joins the normal generative race. Why that hurts in radiology:

* _Patch to vector, precisely._ Each patch's raw pixels (e.g. 14x14x3 = 588 numbers) are linearly
  projected to a vector and given a position embedding; the encoder's transformer layers then mix
  all patches, so each output vector carries whole-image context, not just its own fragment. The
  caption training (CLIP/SigLIP-style) aligns the _whole image_ with its caption -- patches are never
  individually paired with caption words; patch-level meaning only emerges indirectly. A small
  trained projector maps the encoder outputs into the LLM's embedding space. The results are
  continuous points, not vocabulary words.
* _Resolution budget._ Images are downscaled to fit a token budget (Anthropic documents roughly
  width x height / 750 tokens and resizing above about 1.15 megapixels). A mammogram is several
  thousand pixels a side; microcalcifications are a few pixels wide and do not survive.
* _Wrong data shape._ CT/MR are 3D volumes of hundreds of 12--16-bit slices with clinical windowing;
  chat models take a handful of 8-bit 2D pictures.
* _Wrong training signal._ The encoder learned "what caption fits this photo", not pixel-exact
  boundaries. A segmentation model is trained on exactly that boundary, on that modality.
* _Text out._ Findings must be verbalised; the output is sampled language, so the language prior
  can override the pixels (plausible-sounding findings, confident wrong answers -- RadLE 2.0).
* _Not "language" per se._ The transformer is a general sequence model; ViTs are transformers on
  patches, and purpose-trained medical vision models do well on their task. The limit is training
  data, objective, resolution and output form -- not the word "language".

Open: numbers above are mechanism-level; find a citable source for "VLMs answer without looking"
(language-prior shortcut) before putting it on a slide.

=== 2. What exactly is "AI"?

*Rudolph's point.* Today "AI" means LLMs. But most AI that actually delivers clinical value is not
LLM-based: it is old-school pattern matching and classification -- tumour detection, tissue
labelling, segmentation. Calling both "AI" creates a governance mismatch (section 4).

*The surveys prove the point.* The 2026 adoption surveys count "any AI" and never split generative
from predictive cite:[eliciting2026,ama2026]; their top reported uses (ambient notes,
documentation, research summaries) are generative, while what the FDA clears is predictive
cite:[fda_ai_devices_2026]. Even the measurement of AI adoption doesn't define AI.

*Supporting.* The FDA list (imaging-dominated, predictive) versus where generative AI landed
(documentation) makes the split concrete cite:[fda_ai_devices,poon2025]. The Red Hat material adds
the mechanism: a predictive model is a deterministic forward pass from rich input to bounded output;
a generative model samples one admissible output from a distribution, so its probabilism is the task,
not a bug. The two warrant different trust and different oversight.

*Open.* Use the mechanism (forward pass vs sampling) on stage, or keep it to "pattern matching vs
text generation"? The room is technical; the mechanism is a 20-second win.

=== 3. What is computing in a hospital?

*Rudolph's point.* Very little. Until recently, for clinical staff, computers ran Outlook and
scheduling; behind them sat enterprise databases for billing. Using computers for actual computing
barely existed, and still barely does. Forget AI -- hospitals are mostly not equipped for any advanced
computing. The response is to move to the cloud and deploy there.

*Evidence.*

* *Cloud is common but shallow*: a KLAS study reported in July 2025 found more than 80% of health
  systems use a public cloud provider, but few have actually left on-prem cite:[klas2025cloud].
  Small sample (36 systems), so directional only. "On the cloud" usually means some workloads or a
  hosted EHR, not a research computing platform.
* *Predictive vs generative, like for like (on the 3B slide).* Same 2024 AHA survey, both counted
  only if built into the EHR: predictive models 71% of hospitals vs generative (an LLM in the EHR)
  31.5%; by type, system 86 vs 38.5, independent 37 vs 16.3, urban 81 vs 35.5, rural 56 vs 25.4
  cite:[astp2025,everson2025]. The hype is generative; the use is predictive -- and both follow
  money and size.
* *What ASTP's "predictive AI" counts* (from the brief's survey question, checked 2026-10-16):
  "machine learning _or other predictive models_ that display output or recommendations ... in
  your EHR or an app embedded in or launched by your EHR". So it includes non-ML statistical
  scores (APACHE IV is the survey's own example) and operational models (no-shows, billing), and it
  _excludes_ AI outside the EHR -- e.g. imaging AI running in PACS or on the scanner. The 3B slide
  labels it "predictive AI"; strictly it is "EHR-integrated predictive models". Figures verified
  against the brief's table: system 86%, independent 37%, urban 81%, rural 56% (2024).
* *Regional variation -- the data shows a resource divide.* Predictive AI use: system-affiliated
  hospitals 86% vs independent 37%; urban 81% vs rural 56%; critical access 50% cite:[astp2025].
  Generative AI: major teaching hospitals 54% early adopters vs 26% for non-teaching
  cite:[everson2025]. Hospitals that build and evaluate their own models skew well-resourced and
  system-affiliated cite:[nong2025]. No good cloud data by region turned up.
* *Hospitals spend little on IT and lack the skills to use cloud*: IT is about 2.3% of hospital
  operating expense cite:[definitive2025it]; nearly 65% of health systems spend under the
  recommended 5% of revenue cite:[wittkieffer_it]; 43% of healthcare IT leaders name the skills gap
  as the main barrier to cloud cite:[rackspace2025cloud] (vendor survey).

*Open.* Want a number on stage? The resource-divide figures are stronger and more defensible than
the cloud figures.

=== 4. Governance -- a good idea with a challenging application

*Rudolph's point.* This is what happens when well-meaning people who can't define AI must write
policy to govern it. Because "AI" means generative AI, the policies stall or break on anything else.
A small Python model -- about 100 MB, doing tissue segmentation, consuming images and producing images
-- is put through a process that asks about hallucination guardrails. The question is moot: the model
is a deterministic forward pass, not a sampler. Then it stalls on "leaking PHI in text to big model
companies." There is no text and no leak: the model never sees text, sends nothing out, and could run
on a tablet.

*Evidence -- late 2025 (on the slides).* Only 18% of health systems have mature AI governance and a
fully formed strategy cite:[hfma2025gov]; submissions outpace committee decisions nearly 3:2
cite:[elion2025gov]; 29% enforce policies on AI model inventory and sign-off, and only 22% are highly
confident they could produce an AI audit trail within 30 days cite:[blackbook2025gov]. No 2026
governance survey with national scope turned up.

*Background.* Hospitals' own evaluation capacity is thin and uneven: of hospitals using predictive
models, 61% evaluate them locally for accuracy and 44% for bias cite:[nong2025]. Hospitals with
weaker predictive-AI evaluation were _more_ likely to be early generative AI adopters
cite:[everson2025]. Governance itself is thin: in early 2024 only 16% of health systems had a
system-wide AI governance policy cite:[upmc2024gov], and by late 2025 submissions outpaced committee
decisions nearly 3:2, with most programs run by two or fewer dedicated staff cite:[elion2025gov]
(vendor figures). So the scrutiny is pointed at the wrong things: heavy on the small local model,
lighter on the generative tools moving fastest.

*Open.* One concrete before/after anecdote (weeks or months lost on a specific segmentation model)
would carry this section.

=== 5. It's all about the infrastructure

*Rudolph's point.* This is the first real need. Institutions that didn't know computers could compute
now need fairly sophisticated computing. Cloud, job scheduling, containers and orchestration are
alien to IT departments whose job has been installing locked-down Windows on consumer desktops.

*Supporting.* Sections 1--4 converge here: value is in small predictive models (1, 2); hospitals
can't run them (3); governance can't classify them (4). Infrastructure is the common fix. For this
room, this is the familiar part: federated data access, provenance, workflow portability -- the
summit's own cross-cutting list -- with one difference: in medicine the data cannot move.

=== 6. What can be done?

_Claude's draft for Rudolph to cut or replace._

. *Triage governance by what a model is, not what it is called.* A short intake that asks the
  questions that matter: inputs and outputs (images? text?), where it runs, whether anything leaves
  the building, deterministic or sampled, size. A local, image-in/image-out, no-egress predictive
  model gets a fast lane; hallucination and data-leak review is kept for generative and external-API
  tools. Governance stops being one gate sized for the scariest case.
. *Don't make every hospital build a data centre.* Provide computing as shared infrastructure:
  a platform that runs containerised, validated analyses next to the data (ChRIS), on-prem or in a
  research cloud, with audit and provenance built in. The platform carries the compliance work once,
  so each new model doesn't redo it.
. *Move the computation, not the data.* A registry of processable data (HARBOR): find the cohort,
  send the computation to it, return only results -- no images, no PHI. Sites that could never share
  data can contribute, and a small hospital joins with a key, not a data centre.
. *Close the resource divide, don't widen it.* The hospitals least able to compute are the
  independent, rural and critical-access ones cite:[astp2025]. Shared federated infrastructure is
  how they participate; per-hospital build-outs leave them behind.
. *The ask of this room.* Treat hospitals as first-class sites in national data fabrics like NSDF.
  Bring the standards (Musen's talk), the networking (Internet2) and the federation tooling. The
  hospital side brings the data and the clinical questions.

=== 7. The AI Bubble

*Rudolph's point.* Tech conferences rarely mention it, but more analysts now say the numbers don't
make sense: the frontier labs lose money at scale, the financing is circular, neoclouds are
fragile, Oracle is over-leveraged, and the build-out depends on data centres that aren't being
built. Then: what would a crash mean?

*Evidence (Oct 2026).*

* Gartner (14 Oct): AI data-centre owners need ~$8.5T of revenue by 2030 to earn a 12% return;
  forecast ~$3.6T; >80% of neoclouds cease independent operation by 2030; >$3.5T of planned spend
  exposed to impairment cite:[gartner2026neocloud]. Neocloud stocks fell 9--15% on the day.
* IMF GFSR (13 Oct): 14 disclosed AI deals -- ~$300B equity + ~$650B debt for ~$1.4T of promised
  revenue; warns of a correction rippling through a "highly leveraged, circular" sector
  cite:[imf2026gfsr].
* Oracle: 5-year CDS at a record 288 bp (15 Oct); S&P BBB- since July, one notch above junk
  cite:[oracle2026cds].
* Data centres -- CAUTION (checked 2026-10-17): Sightline's Feb 2026 forecast says >=16 GW slated
  for 2026 with ~5 GW under construction cite:[sightline2026], but its page doesn't say US-only
  (Bloomberg, using Sightline data, puts the US share at ~12 GW), and SemiAnalysis (Jun 2026) calls
  the under-construction denominator "off by multiples". No reliable 2026 year-to-date delivered
  figure is public; the only actual is Goldman Sachs' 2.2 GW delivered in Q1 2026 (secondary
  coverage). The slide's "US 16 GW" label and "two-thirds not started" takeaway need fixing.
* Labs: OpenAI projects ~$278B of cash burn 2026--2030 cite:[openai2026burn]; Anthropic reports a
  $65B revenue run rate, with cash-flow breakeven reportedly not before ~2028 cite:[anthropic2026]
  (weak source -- verify). Disclosure: the assistant drafting these notes is made by Anthropic;
  both labs are treated with the same kind of sources.
* Bank of England (Jul): a burst could cut UK GDP by 2.2% cite:[boe2026fsr] (secondary source).
* Ed Zitron has argued this for two years cite:[zitron].

*The premise behind the valuations (on the slides).* Three claims carry the money: AGI is close
("we know how to build AGI" cite:[altman2025reflections]; "a country of geniuses in a datacenter"
cite:[amodei2024grace]); the machines are or will become conscious; they solve what humans can't.
Evidence: frontier models still trail radiologists on hard images cite:[radle2_2026] and finish 46%
of real EHR tasks cite:[liu2026physicianbench]; consciousness science leans cautiously negative on
current AI cite:[consciousness2026]; the Oct 2025 "GPT-5 solved 10 Erdős problems" claim was found
published solutions cite:[techcrunch2025erdos]. Real 2026 results exist (unit distance conjecture
disproved) with mathematicians verifying, refining and publishing cite:[techcrunch2026unitdistance,quanta2026erdos].
Takeaway: a good new tool, priced as if it were a god.
*The maths claim on the slide is Navier--Stokes (Sep 2026).* OpenAI announced an AI-produced proof
of finite-time blow-up for 3D Navier--Stokes -- a $1M Millennium Prize problem -- from >=10,000
agents over ~88 hours, with a 166-page paper and a Lean formalisation cite:[openai2026ns]. Look
deeper: Buckmaster and Alpöge had worked the same gap for a year using LLMs as tools; OpenAI's run
was prompted by rumours of their result and announced ~12 h after they went public
cite:[quanta2026ns,axios2026ns]; a 6 Oct paper found the Lean and written proofs disagree; Clay
lists the problem as active; 28 Fields Medalists warned against treating open problems as
benchmarks cite:[wiki2026nscontroversy]. Disclosure: Alpöge is an Anthropic employee (collaboration
described as personal). The Erdős episodes below stay as background.

For Q&A, the earlier OpenAI maths episodes: (1) Oct 2025 -- "GPT-5 solved 10 Erdős
problems" was literature search; the problems were listed as open on erdosproblems.com only
because the site's maintainer hadn't seen the published solutions. (2) May 2026 -- an internal
reasoning model disproved Erdős's 1946 unit distance conjecture, verified by mathematicians; a
famous problem, not one of the ten, and its "autonomous solution" framing is disputed.

*Earned vs lost (on the slides).* 2025, from leaked draft IPO filings: OpenAI earned $13.1B, lost
$20.9B running the business cite:[openai2025fin]; Anthropic earned $4.6B, lost $8.1B
cite:[anthropic2025fin]. Operating losses used deliberately -- headline net losses ($38.5B, $42B)
are mostly one-off accounting charges.

*What a crash means for this talk (the argument).* The hype and the vendor risk sit in generative
AI; the predictive, local models that actually run in hospitals don't depend on a frontier lab's
balance sheet. A crash would likely leave cheap GPUs behind -- like dark fibre after 2001 -- which
is an opportunity for federated, compute-to-data infrastructure. "Build on what survives the
crash."

=== 8. Conclusions

_Draft._

* The clinical AI that works today is narrow, predictive, often imaging -- and not what policy
  thinks "AI" is.
* Hospitals were built to run care, not computation.
* Governance written for LLMs blocks the models that already work.
* The fix is infrastructure -- shared, federated, compute-to-data -- and governance that can tell a
  segmentation model from a chatbot.

== Format and look

* slidedown, `terminal` theme. Each command + its output is a terminal window (`.term` in
  `talk.sd`); console syntax highlighting on typewriter prompts.
* Needs slidedown `main` at or after FNNDSC/slidedown#1 (merged 2026-10-16): deck CSS beats
  theme, console highlighting, `tw-done`, nexus cursor box, returns keep typed menus.
* Build: `uv run --project ~/src/slidedown slidedown . talk --inputFile talk.sd --theme terminal`.
* Terminal behaviour: the cursor sits at the end of the command while it types, then follows the
  last printed line as bullets reveal. On the hub, the menu prints line by line once
  `talk --outline` finishes, and the cursor parks blinking under it. Up/Down boxes the current
  entry. Relies on slidedown's `tw-done` class (set when a typewriter finishes).
* Presenting keys: digits or Up/Down + Enter pick a section; Right advances; *Backspace* (or Esc)
  returns to the outline; `f` toggles the deck's own fullscreen. In browser fullscreen Esc is
  reserved by the browser (it exits fullscreen), so use Backspace -- especially on the tablet
  (Vivaldi/Android has no Keyboard Lock). From FNNDSC/slidedown#2.
* Bar charts animate on reveal: each bar grows from zero (0.9 s, eased) with a glowing flare riding
  its leading edge that fades once it stops; in the paired chart the generative bar starts 0.25 s
  after the predictive one. CSS only: `.grow` (width transition to `--w`, set by class `wN`) wraps
  a clipped `.fill`; the flare is `.grow::after`. Measured 60 fps with all ten bars animating at
  once (headless, no GPU). Not done: honouring reduced-motion -- deck CSS can't hold `@media`.
* No blank lines inside slide bodies -- slidedown turns them into `<br>`.
* Deck order: title, whoami, whatido, hub (`.id{outline}`), then the spokes, two slides each
  (conclusions one). `transition: zoom` is on: the chosen hub entry flies up to become the
  spoke's heading, so each spoke's first `.h1` must match its menu text.
* Each spoke is three slides: Rudolph's point; the figures (terminal-style bar charts, `.lbl` /
  `.bar` / `.pct` spans); a closing slide whose heading matches the hub entry, so the end-of-spoke
  zoom flies the same words back into the menu. Closing slides carry explicit ids (`<spoke>-end`)
  because a repeated title would collide with the spoke's address. Conclusions is one slide.
* Every figure on a slide has a source line (`.src`) under the terminal; full entries in `refs.bib`.
  Rule: no number on a slide without a source.
* Evidence slides carry three layers of grounding: a subtitle (`.h2`) stating the question the
  chart answers; a dim caption row per chart group saying what is measured, in what units, and
  for whom; and a takeaway bar in plain words. Rule: a chart never appears without saying what it
  measures and why it is there.
* Name the kind of AI every time. Where a source doesn't (most adoption surveys count "any AI"),
  the slide says so.
